# Radical Learning Lab

The app should not copy normal school online. It should behave like a measured learning lab: students play, build, explain, retrieve, collaborate, and earn meaningful benefits only when learning sticks.

## Evidence Anchors

- IES What Works Clearinghouse recommends spacing learning over time, combining graphics with verbal descriptions, connecting concrete and abstract representations, using quizzes for retrieval, and asking deep explanatory questions: https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1
- Education Endowment Foundation identifies high impact for metacognition/self-regulation and feedback, and positive impact for carefully structured collaborative learning: https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit
- Self-Determination Theory argues that autonomy, competence, and relatedness support higher quality motivation, persistence, and creativity: https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/theory/

## Young Learner Model

For K-5, especially K-2, lessons should be built around retention through play rather than screen worksheets.

Lesson loop:

1. Spark curiosity with a story, mystery, sound, image, or real object.
2. Let the child move, sort, draw, build, or act out the idea before symbols.
3. Name the idea in one clear sentence.
4. Retrieve the idea through a fast game.
5. Explain the idea to a parent, sibling, toy, or future self.
6. Revisit it after sleep with a tiny challenge.
7. Unlock a reward only after retention holds.

Design rules:

- Short bursts beat long passive sessions.
- Manipulatives and movement come before abstraction.
- Read-aloud, visuals, and parent prompts are first-class supports.
- Fun is measured through joy, frustration, independence, and return rate.
- A child should feel capable, curious, and in control, not managed by a worksheet.

## Middle And High School Lessons

Every 6-8 and 9-12 lesson should include:

- A meaningful image, diagram, map, data visual, simulation, lab scene, or media prompt.
- An interesting task that requires making, defending, designing, diagnosing, debating, or explaining.
- Structured group homework with 3-5 roles, a shared artifact, and accountability for each student.
- Retrieval checkpoints after 24 hours, 3-7 days, and later spiral review.
- A reward tied to transfer or delayed recall, not just completion.

Group homework pattern:

- Define a shared outcome.
- Assign roles such as data lead, skeptic, designer, reporter, or reflection captain.
- Require every student to explain one piece of evidence.
- Let teams earn recognition for explanation quality, participation, and improvement.
- Avoid competition that makes winning more important than learning.

## Teaching Support Layer

Every lesson should now carry a teaching-support object that is visible in the lesson experience and reusable by the tutor.

Required supports:

- One short learner-facing summary of the key idea.
- One teaching description that explains how the lesson moves from image or object to practice.
- At least three diagram callouts that tell the learner what to look at, build, or explain.
- At least three helper notes for parents, teachers, or the AI coach.
- At least two common misunderstandings with a corrective teaching move.
- One confusion prompt asking the learner to write exactly what does not make sense.

The AI coach should not start by giving an answer. It should ask the learner to write the exact stuck point, analyze the response for visual-model gaps, vocabulary gaps, missing first steps, reasoning gaps, or known misconceptions, then give one targeted reteach move and a retry prompt.

The coach should also let the student choose the help style:

- Find my stuck point for diagnosis.
- Show me a picture for visual or diagram support.
- Tell me a metaphor for story-based explanation.
- Show first step for a minimal scaffold.
- Give real example for transfer.
- Quiz me gently for retrieval practice.
- Start from basics for first-principles reasoning.

Mode results should be logged so the product can learn which explanations improve delayed recall, reduce frustration, and help the student explain the idea in their own words.

The student should be able to grade the tutor response with a quick outcome: helped, still confused, too hard, or needs a picture. The product should treat "still confused" as redesign evidence, not student failure. When feedback is unhelpful, the next tutor response should switch strategy instead of repeating the same mode, and the Fun And Retention Agent should receive a lesson-improvement signal.

First-principles tutor prompts should ask what the learner knows must be true before naming a formula, shortcut, or final answer. For K-5 this means concrete questions like "What whole thing are we looking at?" and "What has to stay fair or equal?" For older learners this means identifying the object, variable, constraint, definition, evidence, and next inference.

## Trial-And-Error Engine

The product should run controlled improvement cycles:

1. Pick a hypothesis, such as "floor movement improves fraction retention for grade 3."
2. Build two safe variants of the same lesson goal.
3. Measure immediate score, 24-hour recall, 7-day recall, joy, frustration, independence, and parent support time.
4. Keep the variant that improves retention without increasing frustration.
5. Retire activities that create fast scores but poor delayed recall.

This means the app should optimize for durable learning, not time spent, page views, or short-term quiz performance.

## Rewards And Benefits

Rewards should support autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

- XP rewards effort, completion, and reflection.
- Mastery badges require delayed recall or transfer tasks.
- Creative unlocks let students choose themes, challenge maps, avatars, music loops, printable missions, or project formats.
- Family benefits are parent-defined non-monetary privileges.
- Crew achievements reward group explanation quality and equitable participation.

Avoid manipulative streak pressure, public shame, pay-to-win advantages, or rewards that teach students to value prizes more than mastery.
